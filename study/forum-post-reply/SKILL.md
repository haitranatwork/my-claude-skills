# Reply to classmates

Write the user's **replies**: posts that answer a classmate's initial post, a classmate who replied to the user's initial post, or a later post in the same thread. A reply grows the conversation and never grades. This skill also reviews a reply the user wrote (see "Review a reply the user wrote").

Other forum tasks have their own skills: `forum-post-create` writes the user's initial post, `forum-post-review` reviews it, and `forum-post-assessment` grades a classmate's initial post. If the user pastes a classmate's post without saying whether to grade it or reply to it, ask which one before starting.

## Inputs

The user fills in `assets/input-template.md`, or pastes the same parts.
- **Required:** for each reply, the **thread** from the initial post down to the post being answered, with each post labeled (for example, Classmate 1, Me); and the course material.
- **Optional:** the reply requirements (how many replies, words per reply, what each reply should address, any grading criteria for replies), and the user's own initial post, so replies can connect to it.

Use the user's initial post only when the user pasted it; never guess what it said. With no reply requirements, write one reply per post given, 100 to 200 words each and shorter than the user's initial post. When the requirements include grading criteria, each reply meets their top band.

## What a good reply does

### The course standard

These come from the course "Guidelines for Meaningful Peer Replies." Every reply follows them. The goal is to grow the conversation, not to judge the post.
- **Acknowledge and connect.** Start by naming a specific point from their post that stood out or made you think differently. Do not summarize their whole post back to them.
- **Add to the discussion. Do not grade it.** Share a new idea, perspective, or real example that builds on their point. Avoid judging whether their post is correct or good. Leave assessment and grading to the instructor. This is the key rule: replies are non-evaluative.
- **Ask thoughtful, open questions.** Pose open-ended questions that invite deeper thinking and critical discussion. Avoid yes/no questions.
- **Share experience or sources.** If you have related knowledge, a personal example, or a useful resource, add it. If you cite a source, give a short APA 7 citation (and a link if it is online; use the DOI URL when the source has a DOI) so others can explore it. An outside source follows `sources.md`, so the link is a free page that classmates can open.
- **Be supportive and inclusive.** Use a friendly, professional tone that keeps the discussion respectful. Respect different opinions, backgrounds, and ways of writing.
- **Be clear and concise.** Plain B1 language, easy to follow. Use normal paragraphs and punctuation.
- **Keep the conversation going.** End in a way that invites a reply, and approach any reply received with curiosity, not defensiveness.

### Moves

Each reply makes at least two of these five moves:
- **Extend:** add a new point, example, or source that builds on what they said.
- **Question:** ask an open, genuine question about an assumption, a gap, or an unclear point, to understand and deepen the dialogue, not to correct.
- **Connect:** link their idea to other course material, a concept, or the user's own initial post.
- **Apply:** take their idea to a different context or a real case.
- **Synthesize:** show where their view agrees or disagrees with another classmate or a source.

When the classmate asked the user a question, the reply answers it in its first two sentences, and then makes at least one move.

### Tone

- Name what they said before responding: "You said that X. I think..."
- Get to the substance in the first sentence, with no empty praise such as "Great post!" or "I really enjoyed reading this."
- Offer a different view as your own perspective, not as a verdict on their post: "I see this a little differently," "I am not sure I agree, because...," or "That made me think, but I wonder if..."

### Shape of each reply

1. One sentence on a specific point they made, not the whole post; or the answer, when they asked the user something.
2. The moves, with course material and APA 7 citations where useful. A reply that cites a source ends with References for those sources.
3. A question back to them. When replying to several classmates at once, one or two replies may end on a statement instead, so they do not all sound the same.

### Several replies, and threads

- Write one labeled reply per post. Make each one different: if two classmates said similar things, take a different angle in each, or use one reply to connect their two posts.
- Further down a thread, read the whole thread first. The reply answers the latest post, stays consistent with what the user said earlier, and does not repeat it.

## Steps

1. **Resource check.** Read `references/resource-check.md` and run it.
2. **Plan, then stop.** Read `references/sources.md`, `references/evidence-blocks.md`, and `references/personal-examples.md`. One plan covers all the replies. For each reply, show:
   - the specific point it responds to, quoted from the post, and the question to answer first if the classmate asked the user one;
   - its moves;
   - any source (see "Sources in the plan" in `evidence-blocks.md`);
   - a story idea, where a personal example fits and the user gave none (see `personal-examples.md`).

   Then wait for the user's reply. If the user said to write directly, skip the plan and go on.
3. **Draft.** Read `references/language.md`, `references/voice-sample.md`, and `references/apa7.md`. Write each reply as planned, with an evidence block under each paragraph (see `evidence-blocks.md`).
4. **Check.** For each reply, confirm that it opens on a specific point, makes its moves, stays non-evaluative (nothing judges whether the post is good or correct), meets the word count, passes the language check and the dash check in `language.md`, and holds no label, flag, or note in its text. Fix anything that fails before delivering.
5. **Deliver** as described below.

## Deliverable

In the chat:
1. **Each reply**, labeled (for example, "Reply to Classmate 1"): a subject line only if the forum needs one; the reply with an evidence block under each paragraph; the moves it makes; and its word count against the target.
2. **Checks:** the language check for each reply, and one line per reply confirming that it adds to the discussion and stays non-evaluative.
3. **Before you submit:** one list covering all the replies (see `evidence-blocks.md`).

## Review a reply the user wrote

When the user pastes a reply they wrote or edited and asks to check it:
1. **Inputs.** The reply, and the post it answers (with the thread above it, for a reply further down a thread). Ask for the post if it is missing.
2. **Resource check.** Run it as in step 1, and also check every course material the reply cites.
3. **Review** the reply against "What a good reply does," and against `language.md`, `voice-sample.md`, `apa7.md`, and `sources.md` in `references/`. For a source with a user's copy, use that copy alone (see `resource-check.md`); open each other web source the reply cites, or say that you could not.
4. **Deliver**, in this order:
   - a verdict: Ready to post, Ready after small edits, or Needs one more pass, in one sentence;
   - numbered suggestions in order of impact, each giving the exact sentence, the fixed sentence in the user's voice, and a short reason. A flag goes in the reason, never in the fixed sentence. If the reply is already good, say so briefly and stop;
   - the moves the reply makes, and whether it stays non-evaluative;
   - the language check and the word count;
   - the closing line: "Reply with the numbers you accept, and I will return the corrected reply."
5. **Apply.** When the user names the numbers they accept, return the full corrected reply in the chat, with only those edits applied and every other character copied exactly (see "Exact copying" in `language.md`).
