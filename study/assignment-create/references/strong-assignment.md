# A strong written assignment

## What the grader looks for

- **A thesis** in the introduction: the answer to the question, or the aim of a report, in one or two sentences. Every section after it supports it.
- **Every part of the question answered**, each in the order the question asks. Top marks on content are usually lost to a missed part, not to weak thinking.
- **Key terms defined** with the course's own definitions, and linked to course concepts.
- **Course material named specifically:** the concept, the author, or the study, never a vague "research shows."
- **Evidence after each main claim:** course material, data, or an example, with a citation.
- **Critical thinking:** ideas or sources compared, strengths and limits weighed, and then the user's own reasoned view. Where the question allows more than one view, the assignment shows them before it takes a position.
- **Specific critique.** A limit that fits any theory ("may not apply to all cultures," "more research is needed") does not count. Name the specific condition where the idea fails.
- **Theory tied to practice:** a concept applied to a real case, such as the user's own work, with the outcome or the lesson stated.
- **One idea of the user's own:** an angle, an example, or a limit that the course material does not hand over.
- **Criteria that are easy to find.** A heading, a topic sentence, or a named concept shows the grader where each rubric criterion is met.

## Structure

1. **Introduction:** the topic in a sentence or two, then the thesis, then the order of the sections. It opens on the topic itself, not on the question repeated.
2. **Body:** one main idea per paragraph. A paragraph opens with a topic sentence that links back to the thesis, gives its evidence with a citation, and says what the evidence shows. At least once, two sources meet in one paragraph: they agree, one extends the other, or they pull in different directions. One source per paragraph, each sitting alone, reads as summary and stays in the middle band.
3. **Conclusion:** the answer to the question in new words, and what follows from it. Every idea and every source in it has already appeared in the body.

Use headings when the instructions ask for them, or when the assignment has sections a reader would look for, as in a report or a case study. A short essay reads well without them. `apa7.md` gives their format.

## Assignment types

The structure above fits every type, and a structure set by the instructions always wins. What changes by type:
- **Essay:** the thesis is a position, and each body paragraph argues for it.
- **Report:** the thesis is the aim of the report. The body runs under headings, such as findings, discussion, and recommendations, and the conclusion answers the aim.
- **Reflection paper:** first person all through. The body tells what happened, which course concept explains it, and what the user learned. It ends with what the user will do differently.
- **Case study:** the introduction names the case and its main problem. The body analyzes the problem with course concepts, weighs the options, and recommends one with reasons.

## Word count

- The target is the range in the instructions. A single number, such as "1,000 words," means within 10% either side, unless the instructions say otherwise.
- The count runs from the first word of the introduction to the last word of the conclusion, with headings and in-text citations. The title, the References list, and the evidence blocks are outside it. A counting rule in the instructions wins.
- Count with code when code can run, because a count by eye drifts.

## The depth ladder

Every claim sits on one of three levels, and the top band lives on level 3:
- **Level 1, repeat:** "Scarbrough (2023) describes five conflict styles."
- **Level 2, explain:** "Collaboration takes more time than the other styles, because both sides must first agree on what the problem is."
- **Level 3, apply and judge:** "Collaboration suited the missed deadline in the team project, because the team had a week to change the plan. It would have failed if a decision had been needed the same day. This suggests that the right style depends on the time available as well as on the relationship."

An assignment whose claims mostly stop at level 1 or 2 is not top band yet. Where the rubric asks for analysis, evaluation, or application, the section that answers it must reach level 3; a section that only explains is summary under another name.

## Why good assignments miss the top band

- One part of a multi-part question is left unanswered.
- Sources are summarized, one per paragraph, instead of used as evidence for the user's own claim.
- The body describes where the rubric asks for analysis or evaluation.
- The conclusion brings in a new idea or a new source.
- References and in-text citations do not match.
- The word count falls outside the range.
