# Voice Sample

Read this before drafting any post. This is the user's real writing. The draft must sound like this.

## What to mirror
- sentence length and rhythm (short, simple sentences)
- level of formality (plain, friendly, academic but not stiff)
- hedging words the user uses ("I think," "maybe," "it seems," "I am not sure")
- how the user opens and closes posts
- first person, personal examples, and questions

Keep the user's common patterns. Do **not** add advanced vocabulary that is not already in this sample. Plain B1 language always wins over the sample posts or any other source.

## Sample (user's own writing)

I think conflict is a normal part of any relationship, and how we handle it will determine where that relationship goes (Scarbrough, 2023). Conflict isn't always bad. It can help us see opposing viewpoints, and when people focus on understanding and resolving the issue instead of blaming each other, it makes the relationship better.

When conflict arises, a very practical and helpful technique is active listening. Scarbrough (2023) explains that listening is completely focused and not interrupting. Listening isn't just about hearing every word, but also about trying to understand the other person's message. This may seem easy, but the reality is that when conflict occurs, we feel uncomfortable, we want to defend ourselves, and we just want to retort. So, I often try to repeat what the other person said before responding. This helps the other person see that I'm listening, and gives us a little more time to de-escalate the tension.

Choosing the right conflict resolution style is also helpful. Scarbrough (2023) explains five styles: avoiding, accommodating, competing, compromising, and collaborating. Collaboration is often best for important relationships because both sides work together to find a solution. It may take more time, but it can protect the long-term relationship. However, collaboration is not always practical. Sometimes avoiding is better when people are too angry to talk. Sometimes compromise is better when time is limited or there is no perfect answer. The key is to choose the style that fits the situation, not just one style every time.

I encountered a conflict during a team project. We had a tight deadline, and one member failed to meet it, causing the entire team to fall behind in phase one. Initially, I felt annoyed. But I decided to ask what had happened first. He explained that a problem arose during his work, but he was hesitant to ask others because he thought everyone was busy, so he tried to figure it out and solve it alone. This took up a significant amount of extra time, even though he had to work overtime at home. My team and I understood the reason and no longer felt annoyed or angry with him. We resolved the issue by revising the plan so that subsequent phases could make up for the delay. Additionally, we agreed that if anyone in the team encounters a problem, they should share the information with the whole team. This way, everyone can help each other more effectively.

Communication helps relationships grow because it creates trust after a problem happens. In my example, the real issue was not only the missed deadline. The deeper issue was that the team member did not feel comfortable asking for help. If he had stayed silent, the team might have blamed him and the relationship could have become worse. However, after we listened to him, we understood his situation and solved the problem together. This helped us create a better rule for the team: when someone has a problem, they should share it early. Ilfiandra et al. (2023) found that conflict resolution counseling helps students build peace values. I think this connects to my example because peace does not only mean there is no argument. It also means people respect each other, control their emotions, and care about the group.
